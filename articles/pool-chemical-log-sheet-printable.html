Pool Chemical Log Sheet (Printable): What to Track Daily

By RouteReady · Professional forms for pool service pros

A chemical log is your paper armor

Every pool you service is a chemistry experiment you don't fully control. The customer tops off the water, the neighbor kid has a party, a thunderstorm dumps nitrogen in — and when the water turns, the first question is always some version of: "what did you put in it?"

A dated chemical log with actual readings answers that question before it's asked. It's the difference between a he-said/she-said argument and a document that shows exactly what the water was doing on every visit. Health departments require commercial pools to keep these logs for a reason. The pros who keep them on residential routes sleep better.

The six readings that actually matter

Free chlorine. The big one. Write the number — 2.5 ppm, not "OK" or a checkmark. Numbers tell a story over time; checkmarks tell nothing.

pH. Target 7.4–7.6. It's the reading customers ask about most, and the one most affected by rain and fresh fill water. Log it every visit.

Total alkalinity. The buffer that keeps pH stable. When a pool's pH keeps swinging, the alkalinity log is where you find the pattern.

Cyanuric acid (CYA). Test it weekly at minimum — monthly for stable pools. High CYA silently eats your chlorine's effectiveness, and it's the culprit behind most "I added chlorine but it didn't work" mysteries.

Calcium hardness. Monthly is fine. Low calcium etches plaster; high calcium scales heaters and salt cells. Either way it's a slow, expensive problem — the log catches it early.

Salt (if applicable). Salt cell pools still need logging. Write the salt reading and the cell's output percentage so you're not chasing phantom low-chlorine events.

How to fill out the log in under a minute

Keep the log pad on your truck clipboard, not buried in a bin. Test the water, read the numbers, write them down before you dose anything — the order matters, because the log should show what the water *was*, not what it is after you fixed it. Then record what you added: product, amount, and which reading you were correcting. One line per visit. Date, tech initials, done.

If you leave a copy with the customer, use the bottom section for notes: "pH creeping up — check the auto-fill valve," or "bromine floater running low, will refill next visit." Customers read these. It makes your weekly visit feel like managed care instead of a drive-by.

What the log protects you from

The most common claim against pool techs is some version of "your chemicals ruined my pool." Without a log, that's your word against a frustrated homeowner. With a log showing stable readings and consistent dosing across twelve visits, the conversation changes completely.

Commercial accounts are stricter. A health inspector can ask for the chemical log on any visit, and "we don't keep one" is a violation in most jurisdictions. If you're servicing HOA pools, apartments, or hotels, the log isn't optional — it's the cost of doing that business. Keep it current, keep it legible, keep it on site.

Stop logging on the back of receipts

A real chemical log sheet has columns for each reading, space for dosing notes, and your company header at the top. It takes the same minute to fill out as a scrap of paper, but it looks like the work of a professional operation — because it is one.
